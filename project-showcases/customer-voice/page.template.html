Of 152 written responses attached to “Too expensive,” 49 mention difficulty paying. Another useful thread: 36 mention delivery flexibility or pausing. I would investigate those friction points alongside price, then test a focused change.

The obvious answer is tempting.

A meal subscription is meant to make the week easier. When someone says it costs too much, a discount seems like a reasonable place to start. But the same answer can hide quite different experiences: not having the money, not getting enough food, or paying for a box that arrives in a week they cannot use it.

I designed the survey around a small structured core, with an open question about what worked and what got in the way. The open question comes first, before suggested reasons can steer the response.

The second sentence changes the conversation. It makes the pause process worth checking. It does not prove that pausing caused the value complaint, or that a discount would fail. That distinction is where the analytical work begins.

One label, several different needs.

Affordability appears in 49 of the 152 comments; value for money in 44. Those are both substantial threads. Neither justifies treating everyone who picked “Too expensive” as if they need the same intervention.

Whose week are we looking at?

Delivery flexibility appears in 16 of 66 price-related comments from people with changing schedules, compared with 2 of 86 from people with regular schedules. Use the schedule filter above to inspect the difference.

It is an association within respondents. The changing-schedule group was also more likely to answer the survey: 140 of 201 invited, versus 163 of 299. Wider intervals cannot repair that selection problem.

The hard part is understanding
what was actually said.

Consider: “I can afford it; I just do not think it is worth the price.” A keyword rule sees afford and price. An analyst has to notice that the speaker is rejecting the affordability explanation.

A small coding experimentKeyword baseline, not a live model
Keyword matches: Affordability; Value for money.

The affordability match misses the negation. This is why a keyword hit is only a candidate for review.

This is where language models can help: proposing several themes, distinguishing praise from criticism, and leaving uncertain meaning unresolved. The useful output is not just a fluent summary. It is a structured assignment that points back to the words.

The pipeline accepts those assignments only when their labels are valid and every quotation exists in the source. That catches invented evidence. It cannot guarantee that a real quotation supports the interpretation, so semantic review remains a separate step.

The next move

Make the next question
more useful.

I would keep price on the table. Explicit budget pressure is too common here to wave away. But I would also investigate the ability to pause or change a delivery: 36 comments raise at least one of those issues, and the supporting passages tell us where to look.

My first step would be a short usability check of the pause flow with customers whose schedules change. If the friction holds up, test a clearer pause experience against the existing one. Decide the sample size from a baseline rate and a minimum worthwhile change, then set the analysis plan before the test starts.
